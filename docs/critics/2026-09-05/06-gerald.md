# Gerald Ashworth-Pryce

Director of a contemporary gallery in London since 1996; has represented three painters from their first studio visit to their estate paperwork.

I looked at Night Shift the way I would look at a portfolio left at the front desk, and I start where I always start, with the ledger. Twelve works. Six commissioned by "Diego", who runs the studio. One by "studio test", one by "e2e", one by "Claude", two by "green alien", one anonymous. That is not a client list. That is a gallery buying its own stock to make the room look sold. The two "green alien" pieces, *After Hours Balance* and *Last Light On*, are the same desk, the same chair pushed back, the same curling sticker, ordered by the same buyer within seven hours. In my trade that is an edition of two, unnumbered, and we would not hang both.

The pricing is the honest part. Three commissions per sender per day, free. A price is a claim about scarcity, and there is none: the output is a 1080 by 1350 PNG on a Vercel blob, reproducible without loss, forever, by anyone. The makers have priced the thing at exactly what it is worth. But do not then put "artist" and "body of work" on the website, because those words carry a price, and the price here is nil.

Now the object, because I sell objects. *Pale Rectangles* is rendered as a photograph of a stretched canvas: you can see the top edge of the stretcher and the white wall behind it. A picture of a painting that does not exist, dressed as a studio snap. *Last Meeting* carries a signature in the lower right, a small brown "R". Nobody in the building is called R. The model has painted in a signature from whoever it learnt from, and nobody caught it. So who signs it? A stranger, by accident. Who is liable for it? I read `lib/artist.ts` and the README, and the answer is a chain: a gatekeeper prompt, an image model called `google/gemini-3-pro-image` reached through a broker called OpenRouter, a cron job, the Instagram API. *The Fence Line, After* was "painted from a photograph sent in by Diego". Had the photograph not been Diego's, who takes the call?

The style. The system prompt says, in so many words, "Your style never changes." I have represented three painters through their whole careers and the one who did not change is the one I dropped. A fixed style is a brand guideline. And it is not even fixed. *After the Toast* is a photoreal still life, thirteen champagne flutes with lipstick on the rims, a stove clock reading 1:37 in green digits, a microwave with a numbered keypad, in a picture whose contract says "no legible words anywhere" and "visible thick brushwork". Set it beside *Pale Rectangles*, flat and chalky, almost gouache, and beside the wedding hall, loaded impasto on the dress. Three hands. What the makers call one style is a paragraph of English posted to whichever vendor is cheapest that quarter: the README says the mood-board was Midjourney and the rendering is Gemini. So the tenth year of this artist is whatever Google ships in the tenth year, with the paragraph unchanged. That is not development. That is a licensing dependency.

As a portfolio, a curator would say it is one painting, done twelve times. Teal wall, amber light, one chair. I counted: a chair or stool appears in ten of the twelve. The makers know it, because they wrote a rule against themselves into the prompt: "never default to a lamp on a wooden desk", "not the same glove, sticker or blank board twice in a day." When a painter needs a memo not to paint the same glove twice, the problem is not the glove. The strongest sheet is *Closed for the Season*, the bulbs over the deck and the towel on the rail, and it is strong because it is least like the others. The weakest is the diner, ductwork, a caution sign, a cash register, and steam still rising from the mug in a picture titled *Last Coffee, Counter Empty*. The README admits the busiest scene was the weakest, and the studio produced another anyway.

And there is no risk. Every painter I have taken on has had a year when nothing sold and the rent was real. Cost to the maker is what a collector actually buys: evidence that someone staked something. Here the growth plan, from the README, is "what I don't paint as asked is kept: it is what the next painter will be made of." Issue #12, painter number two. The artist does not develop; it is replaced. The "daily automated self-critic" is a studio visit conducted by the studio, on the studio. I have sat through those. They end with a glass of wine and no invoice.

## What would make me take it seriously

One. Stop the ledger lying. Take the studio's own commissions off the public wall and show me twelve works ordered by twelve people none of you have met, with names. Until then it is a demo.

Two. One object, one edition, one price. Print the strongest work once, on canvas, destroy the file, and sell it. If nobody pays, you have your valuation. If someone does, you have a market, and we can talk.

Three. Own the signature. Take the "R" off *Last Meeting*, decide who signs, and put that name and a named human's liability on every receipt. A painting nobody can be sued over is a painting nobody stands behind.

Four. Pin the hand. Name the model version on the certificate and refuse to change it for the life of the artist. If the "fixed style" cannot survive a vendor upgrade, admit it is a prompt and drop the word artist.

Five. Show me a second year that is not the first year again. Not a new painter: this one, ten years on, with a period I could hang separately. I do not believe a paragraph can have a late period. Prove me wrong and I will come to the studio.
