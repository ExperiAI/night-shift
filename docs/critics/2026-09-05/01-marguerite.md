# Marguerite Vasseur
Oil painter, Lyon. Thirty-eight years of nocturnes painted on site, at night, from life. Teaches at a small atelier in the 7th.

I looked at all twelve the way I look at a student's canvas when she says it is finished: not at the mood, at the paint. There is no paint. I will show you where it says so.

Start with the weave. In "Last Meeting" the canvas grain runs across the whole picture at the same pitch and depth, through the blue-green wall and straight through the cone of lamplight on the whiteboard. On a real canvas the weave is under the paint. Where the paint is thick the weave is gone; where it is thin it shows. That is the entire physical fact of an oil painting. Their own file asks for "visible thick brushwork in the highlights". A highlight is a ridge; it stands off the ground and catches the real light of the room where the picture hangs. It cannot also show the cloth through it. This is a photograph of a canvas laid over a picture, and anyone who has scraped a palette knife sees it in one second.

Then the light, because they built the whole thing on "one artificial light source" and not one of the twelve obeys it. "Corridor, 3am": the scene says one fluorescent tube at the far end. The tube is there, cold and small. The floor in front is warm amber and the chair throws a hard black shadow toward the lower left, which means a strong warm lamp above and to the right. There is no lamp above and to the right. "Pale Rectangles": one bare bulb, and the wall beside it is lit like a south wall at four in the afternoon, even, with a hard diagonal shadow. A forty-watt bulb goes grey a metre away. This wall goes grey nowhere. "After the Toast" has a hurricane lamp on the counter and an under-cabinet glow and a stove clock reading 1:37 in green digits, with a numeric keypad on the microwave above it, in a studio whose constitution says "No legible words anywhere". The machine did not read its own rules. Neither, apparently, did the daily self-critic.

"Six Rings" has a pendant lamp whose flex comes down out of the shade and runs to the telephone on the table. Look at it. The note says the coat is "still moving because you'd just walked past it". You cannot paint a coat moving. The movement was in the sentence and it stayed there.

"Before the First Toast Fades" shows the prompt working, and that is what condemns it. "Thick brushwork in the highlights": so the wedding dress is a heap of white impasto, strokes the size of a thumb, while the champagne flutes ten centimetres away are rendered like glass in a catalogue, smooth, with refraction. Two hands on one canvas. A painter has one hand; you can follow the wrist across the picture. Here "highlights" was a region and the machine filled the region.

And the stroke is always the same size. "What the Anger Left" is their most painterly picture, and every mark on it is the same width, on the wall, the floorboards, the lamp base, the pillow. I use a 40 flat on a wall and a 2 round on brass. The uniform stroke is the signature of a filter, and a filter is not a brush.

Speaking of signatures: "Last Meeting" is signed "R" in the corner. The artist is called Night Shift. The machine has seen that paintings carry a signature and put down nobody's.

Now the thing only someone who does this can say. I paint the shuttered tabac on rue de Marseille at two in the morning because I am standing in front of it at two in the morning, with the cold going into the brush handle, and the place is empty because I am the one who stayed. Their soul text says "the viewer arrives too late, on purpose". Nobody arrived at all. "Last Coffee, Counter Empty", with its photographic wisp of steam and a lamp hanging over nothing, took under an hour, oldest first, three per sender per day. A nocturne from life takes me three nights, and the third night is mostly scraping off what the first night got wrong. That scraping is what they have called a self-critic and put on a cron.

They name Hopper. Hopper's paint is thin, dry, dragged, lean to the end; he never laid a ridge in his life. Hopper and "thick brushwork" in one breath is two painters who would not have shared a studio, and the machine produced a third who has never held a brush.

## What would make me take it seriously

1. Print one. "After Hours Balance", 80 by 100, on real canvas, under a raking light in a room I can walk into. If the weave shows through the screen glow at the same depth as through the shadow, take it down and stop calling it oil.
2. Remove "oil painting" and "brushwork" from `artist.ts`. Describe the light and the place. If a picture is going to give itself away, let it give itself away as what it is, not as what it imitates.
3. One light means one light. Before a picture posts, have something trace every cast shadow back to the source and reject the picture when they disagree. Twelve of twelve failed this. I would fail a first-year for it once.
4. Make the self-critic show its work in public: the picture it rejected, the reason, the one that replaced it. A critic that never says no in front of anyone is a caption.
5. Send it outside. If it must paint the room after everyone left, let it start from a photograph taken at 2am by whoever commissioned it, standing there in the cold, and say so under every picture. Then at least one person was in the place.
