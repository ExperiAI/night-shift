# Jun Hasegawa
Cinematographer, Tokyo and Berlin. I teach Ozu, Naruse and Kore-eda to film students who would rather be shooting music videos, and I have spent twenty years measuring how high a camera sits.

I read the identity file before the pictures, to see what the painter had been told about my country. It is four words. `Edward Hopper's stillness, Japanese cinema's framing.` That is the entire instruction, sitting in `ARTIST.style` between a note about brushwork and a note about blank signs. A century of decisions, from Ozu's fifty-centimetre lens height to Kore-eda's daylight stairwells, compressed into a token the machine can spend without having paid for it. I think they meant "quiet". But quiet is not a framing, and when you write a country's name into a prompt and ship the pictures, you are borrowing the credit of people who are not in the room.

So I checked, the way I check a student's rushes. Camera height first. In "Last Coffee, Counter Empty" the eye sits a little above a standing adult and looks down on twelve stools, a cash register, a duct, a framed picture on the far wall and a yellow caution sign. In "What the Anger Left" the eye is at standing height again, looking down at an overturned chair. In "Pale Rectangles" it looks down at the box. Every one of the eight I opened is a person on their feet, surveying. Ozu put the camera on the mat and let the room rise above it; the viewer became someone who lived there. Not one of these frames has been sat down in. The one that comes closest, "Corridor, 3am", is worse for it: a one-point corridor with a light at the vanishing point, dead centre, the eye at roughly chair-back height. That is not Tokyo Story. That is the Overlook Hotel.

Then the ratio of empty to full. The style text promises "few objects, one light", and the readme repeats it as the rule that keeps the pictures legible. The bedroom in "What the Anger Left" contains a door, a lamp, a nightstand, a bed, a chair on its side, a crumpled page and a switch plate. The diner contains, by my count, twenty things. Ma is not the space left over after you have finished placing objects. It is the object. These rooms are furnished to be read like a list, and the captions supply the list.

The "one artificial light" is the part I find most foreign, and I mean that literally. Ozu lit interiors flat. He did not want a lamp doing dramatic work; the drama was in the cut. A single warm cone spilling on a wall, as in "Six Rings" or "Last Meeting", is Georges de La Tour with the candle swapped for a pendant. It is beautiful and it is European, and the only thing wrong with it is the label.

Now the sentence I actually came here to argue with. In the departures for "What the Anger Left" the painter writes that "what a feeling knocks over says more than a face ever could". This is the one claim that touches my work, and it is exactly backwards. The vase in Late Spring says nothing on its own. It says everything because Noriko's face was on the screen a second earlier, and because we cut back to her and the face has changed. The empty room is a hinge between two human scenes. Remove the scenes and you do not have a pillow shot; you have a hotel brochure. The painter's soul file says the viewer "arrives too late, on purpose". But no one arrived early. There is no before. A studio that never paints a face has no right to the word "after".

Two smaller things that a cinematographer cannot unsee. The scene text for the bedroom says the door "still trembles slightly", and the hallway coat is "still swinging". A painting does not tremble. Those are film verbs, and they are being used to smuggle duration into a still image that has none, which is precisely the thing a pillow shot has and this does not. And in the bottom right of "Last Meeting" there is a signature, a red "R". Nobody at ExperiAI is called R. The model has painted the initial of whoever it learned this light from and nobody noticed. That, more than any argument, is what these pictures are.

Last: the canvas. `Portrait 4:5 canvas`, hard-coded, because the readme tells us the "Instagram feed dimension is 1080x1350". Ozu shot 1.37, Naruse shot 1.37 and later scope, and horizontal stillness is not a preference; it is the shape a room makes when you sit on its floor. The frame was chosen by the distribution channel. I would not let a student call that Japanese.

## What would make me take it seriously

1. Take the two words out of the prompt. If a film tradition is being cited, cite a shot: "Late Spring, the vase, 1949, camera at floor level, static, twelve seconds". If the makers cannot name one, they should not name the country.
2. Put the camera on the floor. Set a lens height in the render prompt and hold it for a month of paintings. I will look at the horizon line in every frame, and I will know.
3. Paint a face first. If this artist will never paint the human scene, then let a commissioner send one, a photograph, and post it as the first panel, so the empty room is actually after something. Otherwise change the departure text: "says more than a face ever could" is a claim about my cinema, and it is false.
4. Cut the object count to three and mean it. Not "avoid clutter" in a system prompt; a hard limit in the vision check, and a rejection when it is exceeded.
5. Let the canvas be horizontal when the room is. If Instagram is the reason for 4:5, say so on the website, in those words.
