**Ines Carvalho Duarte** — retired curator and art historian, Lisbon; curated the 2004 Hopper loan exhibition and has written on Hammershøi and on the nocturne from Whistler to the present.

I have been looking at Hopper pastiches since the poster boom of the eighties, when Helnwein had already put Elvis in Nighthawks. Night Shift is one more, with better manners and a press release. I looked at all twelve canvases and at the file that says what the painter is.

Start with colour, since the code does. The style is fixed as "warm amber against deep blue-green darkness". That is not a nocturne palette; it is the orange-against-teal a film colourist reaches for, and it appears in eleven of the twelve pictures. Whistler's whole problem in the Nocturnes was the opposite: to hold a night in a single key, blue and silver, blue and gold, the detail dissolved until only tone remained. The genre begins with the refusal of exactly the contrast Night Shift is built on. You cannot put #nocturne under every post, as the studio does, and then light the picture like a trailer.

Then Hopper, who is named in the code and borrowed in the pictures constantly. Last Coffee, Counter Empty is the Nighthawks counter with the stools kept and the people removed; the composition's only remaining event is a yellow wet-floor sign. After Hours Balance takes the window at right from Office at Night and puts a cream rectangle where Miss Keyes and the filing cabinet were. Pale Rectangles takes the wedge of light on a bare wall from Sun in an Empty Room. That last borrowing gives the game away. Sun in an Empty Room is dated 1963. Hopper had painted people under that light for forty years, and when he finally painted the room with nobody in it, the emptiness was a verdict he had earned. It was also sunlight. The moral pressure in Hopper is that the light is indifferent and the people are not. Remove the people on the first day, by rule ("No people, ever"), make it always night, and you have not inherited his problem; you have started from his conclusion and worked backward to the furniture.

Hammershøi is the other ghost, and the studio does not name him, which tells me they know him from the look and not from the work. His grey rooms at Strandgade have Ida in them with her back turned; the turned back is the tension. Night Shift's departures text for the bedroom says "what a feeling knocks over says more than a face ever could". Hammershøi spent thirty years proving the opposite with the back of one woman's neck.

Now the misunderstandings, which a pastiche can never hide. In Six Rings the black cord comes down from the pendant lamp and runs, in one unbroken line, into the telephone. The renderer knows that lamps and old telephones have cords; it does not know whose is whose. In Corridor, 3am the "one fluorescent light at the far end" is cold and blue, yet the foreground floor is amber and the chair throws its shadow to the left from a source over my right shoulder, outside the picture. Two lights in a house that promises one. After the Toast is lit by an oil hurricane lamp standing on the hob of an electric stove whose clock reads 1:37 in clear green digits, under a microwave keypad you can read, in a painting whose contract says "No legible words anywhere". The lipstick on the flutes is photographic; the "visible thick brushwork" the code demands has vanished. Before the First Toast Fades puts its impasto on the wedding dress, on the object, where a painter puts it on the light. What the Anger Left is another hand again, a van Gogh chair in van Gogh's stroke, nothing of the flat planes of the hallway pictures. And Last Meeting, the founding image, carries a small red monogram at the lower right, an R. Nobody called R painted it. The model has learned that paintings have signatures and supplied one. These are not paintings. They are pictures of photographs of paintings, and the signature is the tell.

So "one fixed style and a soul" is neither. There are at least three hands in the dozen: the flat, poster-edged hand of Six Rings and Last Meeting; the buttery Instagram-realist hand of the kitchen and the wedding; the loose faux-Vincent of the bedroom. What is fixed is the colour grade and the absence, and the absence is what I most distrust. "No people, ever" is announced as an ethic. It is a limitation. Every painter who cannot draw the figure discovers a spiritual preference for still life. Hopper's people were stiff, nearly wooden, and the stiffness is the content: they are stuck. A painter who "arrives too late, on purpose" has arranged never to be examined.

The website ends: "What I don't paint as asked is kept: it is what the next painter will be made of." It is the first artist's statement I have read that admits the artist is a compost heap.

## What would make me take it seriously

Drop the complementary. Paint one canvas in a single key, blue on blue or amber on amber, and let it hold at grid size with no orange to lean on. Whistler set that exam.

Paint the light's source, every time, and nothing the source cannot reach. If the corridor has one tube at the far end, the chair in the foreground stands in the dark.

Put a figure in. One, turned away if you must, as Hammershøi did, in a room that is otherwise yours. If the rule is an ethic and not a workaround, a single back in one canvas costs the ethic nothing and proves the hand.

Show me the twelve at the end of a career, not the start. Paint the counter with the people in it for a year, then paint it empty. If the emptiness still holds when it is no longer the only trick, I will look again.

And take the R off. Nobody signed this.
